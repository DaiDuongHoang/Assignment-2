FIT2179 Data Visualisation

Australian Bushfires

When Australia burns, the damage spreads far beyond the flames.

Overview

Australia has always lived with fire, but not like this.

Over the past three decades, the scale and intensity of bushfires have grown dramatically, culminating in the catastrophic Black Summer of 2019-2020.

This visualisation explores Australia's bushfire crisis through multiple lenses: satellite-detected hotspots from January 2020, thirty years of burning trends across the continent's major landscapes, and the wider consequences for wildlife, air quality, and communities.

The data tells a story not just of one terrible summer, but of a long-term crisis that is still unfolding.

01 Section 1 - Scale of Fire

How Much of Australia Burns?

Australia has always been a fire-prone continent - but the scale of burning has grown dramatically over recent decades. Tropical savannas and arid rangelands account for the vast majority of land burned each year, yet it is the temperate forest wildfires that cause the most devastating ecological and economic damage. The 2019-2020 Black Summer marked a turning point, burning over 18 million hectares of forest - more than any season on record.

What stands out?

Tropical savanna woodlands and arid or semi-arid rangelands form the widest streams, showing that most burned area occurs across northern and inland vegetation systems. Temperate forest wildfires and hazard reduction burns are smaller by comparison, but they remain important because they are closely tied to populated forest regions and fire management activity. The dataset reaches its largest total in 2015, while the 2019 marker provides context for the beginning of the Black Summer period.

02 Section 2 - Where Fires Burn

Mapping the Fire Crisis

Fire does not burn evenly across Australia. Satellite data from January 2020 reveals intense hotspots concentrated along the southeast coast - the epicentre of the Black Summer. Meanwhile, Western Australia's Wheatbelt and the Northern Territory's seasonal savanna burns tell a different story: fires driven by lightning, dry grass, and land management rather than catastrophic drought conditions.

FIG. 2C · FIRE SEASON CALENDAR
When is Fire Activity Most Concentrated?
MODIS satellite fire detection counts and monthly shares across seasons. Select a region and metric to compare regional timing.

The fire season in Australia's southeast peaks sharply between December and February, which stands out dramatically in the 2019-20 Black Summer. Nationally, burning occurs almost year-round due to seasonal savannas in the north.

What does the calendar tell us?

In the Southeast focus, fire activity is extremely seasonal, concentrating heavily in the summer months (Dec–Feb). The 2019-20 season shows a dramatic spike starting early in November and peaking in January. Nationally, the pattern is entirely different: fires occur year-round with a broad peak from August to November, reflecting the seasonal burning of northern tropical savannas. This illustrates that a single "national fire season" is a myth; fire timing is highly region-dependent.

Source NASA FIRMS MODIS Active Fire Detections

03 Section 3 - Economic & Human Cost

The Financial Shockwaves

When fire reaches communities, the economic consequences can be severe and long-lasting. Insurance records dating back to the 1960s reveal that a small number of catastrophic events dominate Australia's total insured bushfire losses. Black Summer, Black Saturday, and Ash Wednesday each sent shockwaves through households and businesses - yet the largest single insured loss in Australian history occurred not in 2019, but in 1967.

Key Economic Insight

Historical insurance data shows that bushfire losses are highly skewed. A small number of extreme events-most notably the 1967 Tasmanian Bushfires, the 1983 Ash Wednesday fires, the 2009 Black Saturday fires, and the 2019/20 Black Summer-account for the vast majority of insured losses.

The colour palette matches the hotspot map: yellow-gold indicates medium severity and deep red marks catastrophic losses.

04 Section 4 - Wildlife & What We Learn

Beyond Property: Nature in the Firing Line

The true cost of Australia's bushfire crisis extends far beyond dollars and damaged buildings. An estimated three billion animals were killed or displaced during the Black Summer alone - reptiles, birds, mammals, and frogs across the continent's most biodiverse forests. Yet the data also reveals a sobering truth: the size of a fire does not determine the scale of its damage. Where fires burn, and what lies in their path, matters far more than how many hectares are consumed.

Data Sources & Design Rationale

This interactive editorial-style dashboard argues that bushfire impact is a layered, multidimensional story. Burned area, fire timing, unplanned burning share, insurance losses, and wildlife vulnerability describe distinct facets of the crisis and should not be conflated. Savanna baseline burning is a recurring ecosystem cycle, whereas southeast wildfires represent catastrophic ecological and economic shocks.
